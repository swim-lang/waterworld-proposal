Design system & brand standards proposal

Everybody working from the same Water World.

Your marketing team, in-house designer, agency, and partners are all making things for the park, each a little differently. We'll give everyone one shared design system, so everyday decisions get easier, the materials feel like they belong together, and good ideas still have room to land.

    1 Start here

    A lot of people making things. A few too many versions of the brand.

    Your answers to our questions helped clarify the assignment. Water World already has a recognizable logo and decades of brand equity. The practical challenge is getting everyone creating materials to work from the same visual decisions.

    Different contributors are using different fonts, layouts, motifs, and patterns, and the park's mascot and characters show up in different ways. Your marketing team manages direction and approvals, but there isn't one clear system everyone can reliably work from.

    We'll build that system around the strongest parts of the existing brand: logo, color, type, motifs, patterns, and mascot, organized into a design language that's logical and easy to follow. Typography is a real priority: it's where much of the current inconsistency starts, so it gets early attention, and the answer is usually fewer typefaces, chosen well. The aim is one definitive source of truth, and a brand people recognize as Water World even before they see the logo. The work will support your primary audience of Colorado and regional families while allowing campaigns for groups, tourists, and young adults to adjust their tone and emphasis.

    A good system also knows what to leave out. We're not trying to produce a 50-page book. We'd rather give you fewer, better decisions, plus clear guidance for adding to the system later, so when Water World needs something new there's already a sense of what fits and what doesn't.

    The standards should help someone choose a font, build a layout, brief a vendor, or review an ad with more confidence. They should also improve the materials themselves: clearer hierarchy, better relationships between elements, and a more consistent expression of the brand you already have.

    How each phase works

    01Understand the starting point

    We'll begin with a three-hour kickoff workshop. We'll spend as much time on the outcomes Water World wants as on the problems in front of you, including business goals that reach beyond this project's deliverables. Understanding where the park is headed helps us anticipate what you'll need next, and that's the kind of long-term relationship we want to build.

    We'll also gather focused input from marketing, the internal designer, a social/content contributor, and an agency representative. We'll review a representative collection of current and recent materials: website graphics, organic and paid social, display ads, email, outdoor, print, signage, video, promotional pieces, merchandise, and work produced by both staff and partners.

    The audit will identify what is already consistent, what keeps changing, and where people need clearer direction. We'll distinguish assets with established equity from elements that could benefit from refinement. Water World's existing logo is the starting point for its usage standards.

    We'll also review available historical materials. Nostalgia can inform our choices when it connects the work to something meaningful and familiar. We'll consider how those references read to first-time visitors as well as people who grew up with the park.

    02Develop the system

    Typography is one of the first pieces we'll settle, because so much of the current inconsistency starts there. We'll establish clear roles for primary, secondary, and any supporting or display fonts, including headlines, subheads, body copy, and calls to action. Recommendations will consider readability, accessibility, licensing, web use, and practical availability in Canva and Adobe. We'll specify substitutes where the main fonts aren't available, including email environments.

    We'll formalize logo usage and the color palette, then develop a focused graphic language. That may include shapes, patterns, textures, illustration direction, icons, frames, and backgrounds, with water or Colorado references where they serve the work. The aim is a visual language strong enough that a post, poster, or sign reads as Water World even when the logo isn't the dominant element. The park's existing mascot and characters are part of that language, and we'll define how they're used; a new mascot program would be a separate scope.

    We'll present two focused visual approaches using representative applications, then develop one selected direction. Exploration will draw from the audit and useful historical references, keeping the system practical for today's production needs.

    03Put it to work

    We'll test the selected direction across digital and physical applications, including short and long headlines, different photography, seasonal promotions, and informational messages.

    We'll define the elements that stay consistent and the elements contributors can vary. A social post, billboard, and operational sign have different jobs; the system needs to account for those differences.

    Photography and video direction will cover subject matter, guest representation, attractions and lifestyle imagery, composition, movement, lighting, color treatment, authentic and staged content, drone imagery, titles, overlays, and social-first video. Examples will show how professionally produced and internally captured content can work together.

    Basic voice guidance will address personality, headline style, calls to action, humor, and the differences between promotional, informational, and operational messages.

    04Make the handoff useful

    We'll organize the final standards and assets so staff and partners can find the current files and understand how to use them. The guide will include practical examples and common mistakes alongside specifications.

    We'll conduct a 60-minute walkthrough for staff and agency partners, with time for questions. The transition plan will prioritize social, paid advertising, email, website creative, agency-produced work, and internal templates. Permanent signage, remaining print stock, merchandise, and environmental graphics can transition as replacement becomes appropriate.

    5 Try the tool

    Some design decisions only need to be made once

    Canva and Adobe templates are part of this engagement, and they'll do a lot of the work. Alongside them, we're building our own software for managing design systems: tools with a brand's rules built in, so decisions about layout, type, and color are made once and hold up every time someone makes something new.

    The sample below is a simple version of that idea. You change the content: the headline, the photo, the date, and the details. The tool takes care of the design: type sizes, spacing, colors, and logo placement adjust on their own and stay inside the approved system. Someone who isn't a designer can make an on-brand promotion in a minute, and your designers get that time back for work that needs them.

    Because it's our own software, it can be shaped around how your team actually works, which off-the-shelf tools never quite manage. It also scales: more templates, more formats, and eventually AI-assisted workflows that check work against the standards. We think this is where design systems are heading. Depending on how far Water World wants to take it, some of this may sit outside the base engagement, and we'd love to talk through what's possible.

    Try it: type a new headline, pick a photo, change the colors or add a pattern, and watch the design hold together.

    Your questions, answered

    1Based on your experience, what separates a useful brand standards guide from one that ultimately sits unused?

    Simplicity, and a compass.

    Senior designers get very good at hitting delete. A useful guide makes fewer, clearer decisions, so people can remember them, and it answers the questions that come up while making things: which font, how much flexibility, where the files live.

    The other difference is a big idea behind it. Too many standards documents are a list of rules with no compass, so nobody knows what to do when a situation isn't covered. For Water World, we think that compass could be nostalgia: decades of summers and the memories people bring with them. It can make the standards feel clear and a little aspirational, and it gives people a way to judge new work.

    2How would you approach refining an established brand without making it feel like a complete rebrand?

    We start with return on investment. We're a business-focused agency, so every decision gets weighed for its risks, rewards, and consequences before anything changes. The logo, which your team identified as the strongest recognizable asset, stays the anchor; we'll clarify its use and improve the system around it.

    Then, honestly, it's about being clever: finding the small moves that make a big difference, rather than changing things for the sake of it.

    It's also about timing. We may have some ambitious ideas for where the brand could go, but they don't need to happen on day one. We'll separate what needs to work now from a roadmap for later, so the refinement feels like progress, not a reset. Any significant logo change would be explained and scoped separately.

    3How do you develop a visual identity that remains recognizable while allowing enough flexibility for seasonal campaigns, events and promotions?

    Usually this has less to do with the design system than with context. The system doesn't need to pivot for every season or promotion; photography, messaging, and campaign ideas can do that work while the core stays put: logo use, type, color, and graphic language.

    There's also a bigger opportunity. Water World's season is part of what makes it special, and the brand can have fun with that rather than work around it. We'll show where campaigns can flex and give contributors clear boundaries with plenty of room to play.

    4How would you approach Water World's current typography inconsistency?

    Honestly, we don't know yet, and we'd be wary of anyone who claims to before looking.

    Getting it right means taking inventory of every font in use, understanding the pain points for the people setting type, checking licenses and what's available in Canva and Adobe, and, most importantly, understanding the why behind the choices people are making today. That takes a few real conversations, not a guess in a proposal.

    What we can say is that consistent typography usually comes from fewer typefaces, chosen well, with clear roles for headlines, body copy, and calls to action, plus substitutes for when the main fonts aren't available. Knowing what to choose is where the skill is. Sometimes the right answer is an existing typeface, lightly customized so it feels ownable.

    5How do you account for the different needs of professional designers, marketing staff, social media teams and external agencies when developing brand standards?

    We'd go beyond a PDF. The comprehensive PDF guide is included, as the RFP asks, but a PDF treats everyone the same, and a designer, a social media manager, and an agency partner need very different things.

    Our recommendation is a web-based guide that recognizes who's using it and puts what that person most likely needs first: precise specs and source files for designers and agencies, quick guidance and templates for marketing and social, production specs for vendors, with everything else still a click away. It's the same thinking as the tool in this proposal, and we'd scope it with you.

    6What steps do you take to ensure a new visual system can be practically implemented across an organization?

    Testing, testing, and more testing. A lot of agencies present hypotheticals without mocking the system up in real-world situations. We'll put it to work on real Water World applications, with real headlines, photos, and constraints, and test it with the people who'll use it.

    We'll also be upfront that systems evolve. Once something is out in the world, some pieces will need a tweak, and that's normal. Expecting it puts everyone in a much better position than pretending it won't happen.

    Then we'll deliver organized assets, train staff and partners, prioritize the most immediate applications, and let longer-lived physical materials transition at sensible replacement points.

    7What involvement would you need from Water World staff during the project?

    Enough to understand your point of view, your history, and where you want to go: a marketing lead to coordinate and consolidate feedback, focused input from the internal designer, a social/content contributor, and an agency representative, and park leadership at agreed checkpoints.

    Beyond that, we pride ourselves on being white glove. It's collaborative, but our job is to solve your problems without making you feel like you're under the hood of the car with us the whole time. You shouldn't finish this project feeling like you did it yourselves.

    In practice: a three-hour kickoff, one direction presentation, two consolidated direction feedback rounds, one guide/template correction round, and a 60-minute training session, with feedback within two business days to keep the December 23 target.

    Thoughtfully, no. The scope generally lines up with what you've described, and we wouldn't feel confident changing it without a more in-depth conversation.

    How we usually work is to reverse-engineer deliverables from a company's goals. You've shared yours, and we'd like to go further in our early conversations. If what we learn points to a different mix of deliverables, we'll say so before anything changes. Extensive consumer research, asset and signage refreshes, and comprehensive positioning stay outside this engagement, as you clarified.
